Keeping Your Lake Oswego Water Heater Running Longer

The best way to avoid a surprise leak is routine maintenance. We recommend that homeowners flush the tank once a year (more often with hard water), have the anode rod checked every three to five years, lift the T&P valve lever once a year to make sure it isn't stuck, and keep the thermostat around 120 degrees, which limits scalding risk and slows mineral buildup. Tankless owners should plan on descaling every year in hard-water areas.

Age matters too. Most tank water heaters last 8 to 12 years, tankless units often reach 20 with regular descaling, and heat pump models usually run 10 to 15. If your heater is past that window and showing symptoms such as rusty hot water, popping and rumbling, dampness under the tank, or showers that go cold sooner, a planned replacement is almost always cheaper and much easier on you than an emergency one after the tank lets go.

The earlier you report a hot water problem, the cheaper it usually is. A sediment layer that's just starting to rumble can often be fixed with a flush, yet that same buildup, neglected for a few years, can overheat the bottom of the tank and burn out the lower element on an electric model, turning a simple visit into a replacement. Save our number on your phone today so you can reach us fast as soon as something sounds or looks wrong. Early intervention saves time, money, and ruined floors.

Hot Water Headaches Lake Oswego Homeowners Face

Water heaters in and around Lake Oswego deal with a familiar set of problems. Depending on the neighborhood and the water source, hard water may be a real factor: calcium and magnesium drop out as sediment on the tank floor, insulating the water from the burner, and that's what causes the rumbling and popping you may hear when the heater fires. Tankless units aren't immune either: scale builds inside the heat exchanger, and that's why annual descaling matters so much where the water is hard.

Age is the other big factor. Many homes in the area still have the original tank, and once a tank is past about ten years, the anode rod is usually spent and corrosion starts working on the steel from the inside. Homes here also run on a mix of fuel types, natural gas and propane units in some homes and electric in others, so our technicians handle everything from failed thermocouples and draft problems to burned-out elements and failed thermostats.

Code is another piece. Replacing an older heater often means updating the installation along the way: a thermal expansion tank if the water system is closed, a T&P discharge line that terminates correctly, a flue that drafts correctly on gas units, plus straps and a pan where required. Homes on well water have their own issues, such as heavier sediment, iron staining, and the rotten-egg smell that occasionally develops when certain water chemistry reacts with a standard anode rod. All of these is fixable, and most are much cheaper when caught early. If your water heater is doing anything unusual, a quick diagnostic visit catches the small problem before it becomes an expensive one. Give us a call at (503) 210-1622 to set up a visit.
