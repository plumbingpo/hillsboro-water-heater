A gas water heater is a fairly simple machine, which is part of why it lasts. A burner under the tank heats the water, exhaust rises through a flue that runs up the center of the tank, and a gas control valve on the front acts as both thermostat and safety shutoff. That valve only lets gas reach the burner while it senses a flame, through a thermocouple on standing-pilot models or a flame sensor on electronic ignition models. Most failures trace back to one of those parts. In Hillsboro, OR, we regularly find thermocouples that no longer hold the valve open, pilots starved by a lint-clogged flame arrestor screen, gas valves that have failed internally, and flue or vent problems that let exhaust spill back into the room.

Diagnosing a gas water heater properly means checking combustion, not just parts. Across Hillsboro, our technicians measure thermocouple output in millivolts, study the pilot and burner flame for color and shape, read gas pressure at the valve, and test draft at the hood so we know exhaust is leaving the house. We check the combustion chamber for soot and scorching, which point to a blocked flue or poor combustion air, and we test for carbon monoxide near the heater. Safety always comes first: a gas smell means leaving the house and calling the gas utility from outside before anything else, and Hillsboro Water Heater can take over from there.

Most gas water heater repairs are finished in a single visit. We replace thermocouples, flame sensors, pilot assemblies, and igniters, clean burners and flame arrestor screens, swap failed gas control valves, and correct vent connectors that have separated or lost their slope. We also flush sediment, the cause of that popping or rumbling sound and a major reason tanks overheat at the bottom and fail early. When the tank itself is leaking or the heater is deep into its service life, we tell you honestly and walk you through replacement. New installations are sized by first-hour rating, not just gallons, and include the right venting, a sediment trap on the gas line, an expansion tank where needed, and seismic straps and a drain pan where code requires them. How to Tell Something Is Wrong

Recognizing the warning signs early can save you money and keep a slow drip from turning into a flooded utility room. If any of the following sound familiar, it's worth having the unit checked:

  • Popping, rumbling, or crackling sounds as the burner heats
  • Less hot water than the tank should deliver
  • Any smell of gas around the appliance, which calls for leaving and phoning the gas utility
  • A pilot light that will not stay lit
  • A silent heater with no flame and no hot water
  • Rust-colored hot water or rust streaks on the tank
  • A vent pipe that has separated, rusted, or left scorch marks near the top of the heater
  • Soot or scorch marks around the burner access door

What a Trained Technician Brings to the Job

Gas water heaters are among the riskiest appliances to work on without training, largely because you cannot see the biggest hazards. A gas connection that is not quite tight can leak slowly for days. A vent connector that has lost its slope, or a flue partly blocked by debris, can let carbon monoxide spill into the house every time the burner fires, with no smell to warn you. Gas valve and heater replacements also typically require a permit and an inspection. Relighting a pilot by following the label is something most homeowners can do; almost everything past that deserves a technician.

Our technicians approach gas heaters with instruments rather than assumptions. They check thermocouple output in millivolts, read gas pressure at the valve, test draft at the hood, check for CO around the appliance, and leak-test every joint they touch. The result is a repair that targets the real fault and a home that is safer for it. We also give you an honest assessment of the heater itself. A tank past about ten or twelve years with a failing valve or a leak is usually a replacement, and we will say so before you spend money on a repair.

Tips to Keep Your Water Heater Healthy

Most of the failures we see could have been delayed for years with basic upkeep. Start with these practical measures our team relies on:

  • Make sure your home has CO alarms that work, especially with gas appliances
  • Watch for a loose, rusted, or sagging vent pipe above the heater
  • Never store flammable liquids near a gas water heater
  • Flush a few gallons from the drain valve periodically to control sediment
  • Vacuum lint and dust from the flame arrestor screen at the base a few times a year
  • Set the gas valve near 120 degrees, because hotter water costs more and can scald
  • Have the anode rod inspected every few years and replaced when it is worn
